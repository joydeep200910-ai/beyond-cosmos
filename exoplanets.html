The Basics

What Is an Exoplanet?

An exoplanet is a planet that exists outside our Solar System. These worlds orbit stars other than the Sun and come in an incredible variety of sizes, temperatures, and compositions.

Some are rocky like Earth, while others are enormous gas giants. Scientists have discovered thousands of these distant worlds, revealing just how diverse planetary systems can be.

Worlds Beyond Our Sun

Every exoplanet gives astronomers another opportunity to understand how planets form, evolve, and possibly develop the conditions needed for life.

A Universe of Worlds

Types of Exoplanets

Exoplanets come in many forms. Some are rocky, some are enormous gas giants, and others may be completely unlike anything in our Solar System.

>
01
Rocky Worlds

Terrestrial Planets

Rocky exoplanets are worlds made primarily of rock and metal. Some may have surfaces and atmospheres that resemble Earth.

02
Extreme Worlds

Hot Jupiters

These enormous gas giants orbit extremely close to their stars, producing incredibly high temperatures.

03
Larger Than Earth

Super-Earths

Super-Earths are planets larger than Earth but generally smaller than Neptune. Their composition can vary dramatically.

04

Ocean Worlds

Some distant planets and moons may contain enormous quantities of water beneath their atmospheres or icy surfaces.

05
Gas & Ice

Mini-Neptunes

Smaller than Neptune but larger than Earth, these planets may possess thick atmospheres surrounding rocky or icy interiors.

Finding Distant Worlds

How Do We Detect Exoplanets?

Most exoplanets are too distant and faint to see directly. Instead, astronomers look for the subtle effects these worlds have on their stars.

01

Transit Method

When a planet passes in front of its star, it blocks a tiny amount of starlight. Telescopes measure this small decrease in brightness to reveal the planet.

Measuring Starlight
02

Radial Velocity

A planet's gravity makes its star move slightly. Astronomers detect this tiny stellar wobble by measuring changes in the star's spectrum.

Stellar Wobble
03

Direct Imaging

In rare cases, powerful telescopes can capture light coming directly from an exoplanet after suppressing the overwhelming glare of its star.

Seeing the Planet
04

Gravitational Microlensing

A planet's gravity can bend and magnify light from a more distant star. This brief change in brightness can reveal an otherwise hidden world.

Bending Light
The Search For Life

The Habitable Zone

Not every planet is at the right distance from its star. The habitable zone is the region where temperatures could potentially allow liquid water to exist on a planet's surface.

HABITABLE ZONE
Conditions may allow
liquid water

Too Hot

A planet too close to its star may become extremely hot, causing surface water to evaporate.

Potentially Habitable

At the right distance, temperatures may allow liquid water to remain on the surface.

Too Cold

A planet too far from its star may become cold enough for surface water to freeze.

Important: Being inside the habitable zone does not automatically mean that a planet supports life. Atmosphere, chemistry, planetary geology, radiation and many other factors matter.

The Search For Life

Could Life Exist There?

Finding an exoplanet in the habitable zone is only the beginning. Scientists need to study many other conditions before determining whether a world could potentially support life.

01

Liquid Water

Water is considered one of the key ingredients for life as we know it. Scientists search for environments where liquid water could persist.

02

Atmosphere

An atmosphere can influence temperature, climate, surface pressure and the chemical environment of a planet.

03

Temperature

A planet's temperature depends on its star, atmosphere, orbit and other factors. Extreme conditions can make surface environments hostile.

04

Stellar Radiation

Powerful radiation from a host star can affect planetary atmospheres and the conditions on a planet's surface.

05

Chemical Ingredients

Scientists investigate the presence of elements and molecules that could participate in the chemistry associated with life.

06

Biosignatures

Certain atmospheric or surface features could potentially provide clues about biological activity, although detecting a true biosignature requires careful scientific analysis.

Habitability is not the same as life.

A planet may have conditions that appear favorable for life without actually containing life. Scientists therefore examine multiple lines of evidence before drawing conclusions.

The Next Chapter

The Future of Exoplanet Exploration

We have only begun to discover the worlds beyond our Solar System. New observatories and improved techniques are opening the door to a much deeper understanding of distant planets and their atmospheres.

01

Studying Atmospheres

Astronomers can analyze starlight that passes through a planet's atmosphere to search for gases and learn more about its composition.

02

More Powerful Telescopes

Next-generation observatories will improve our ability to detect smaller planets and investigate distant planetary systems.

03

Searching for Biosignatures

Future observations may allow scientists to investigate atmospheric combinations that could provide clues about biological processes.

04

Discovering New Worlds

As detection methods improve, astronomers are expected to find an even greater diversity of planets across the Milky Way.

The search has only just begun.

Every new exoplanet gives us another piece of the puzzle of how planetary systems form, evolve and potentially create environments suitable for life.
